SCQ 1Persistent pain after childbirth

Q1.1Definition, and why the reported incidence varies so widely

  • IASP / ICD-11 chronic post-surgical pain: pain that develops or increases after surgery or tissue injury, persists beyond healing (more than 3 months), is localised to the surgical area or referred to the territory of a nerve there, and has other causes excluded
  • Ranges worth knowing: caesarean scar pain roughly 7 to 31% at 3 months and 2 to 19% at 6 months; perineal pain after vaginal delivery roughly 4 to 56% at 3 months and 2 to 6% at 6 months

Q1.2Impact of persistent pain after childbirth

  • Physical function: lifting and carrying (including the baby), stairs, rising from a chair, walking, sport. Impairment tracks pain intensity
  • Sleep, mood and care of the baby, including bonding
  • Association with postpartum depression, and with higher healthcare use and socioeconomic cost
  • Pelvic or perineal pain and dyspareunia can affect relationships and are often under-reported. Pain can also affect breastfeeding positions and return to work

Q1.3Proposed pathophysiology

  • Thought to follow the general chronic post-surgical pain model, but evidence specific to childbirth is limited
  • Transition from acute to persistent pain: tissue injury drives nociceptive and inflammatory input, and inflammatory mediators sensitise nociceptors (primary hyperalgesia)
  • Peripheral sensitisation (changes in gene expression in DRG neurones) and central sensitisation, where nociceptive responses become decoupled from peripheral input. Persistent pain may be a hyperalgesic state that fails to resolve after healing
  • Neuropathic component: nerve entrapment with a Pfannenstiel incision (ilioinguinal or iliohypogastric nerve)
  • Oxytocin may be protective. In animal models spinal oxytocin rises after delivery and during lactation, intrathecal oxytocin reverses nerve-injury hypersensitivity (blocked by atosiban), and hypersensitivity returns after weaning. Human data are limited

Q1.4Risk factors

  • Pre-existing pain, especially back pain and migraine
  • Psychological: depression, anxiety, stress, catastrophising, psychological vulnerability. Also social deprivation and poor access to care
  • Severe acute postpartum pain: a consistent predictor (about 2.5-fold risk at 8 weeks, whatever the mode of delivery)
  • Surgical and anaesthetic: repeat Pfannenstiel incision (more fibrosis and nerve entrapment), closure of the parietal peritoneum. General anaesthesia is conflicting, and emergency caesarean section is inconsistent across studies
  • Genetics: OPRM1 A118G is linked to higher opioid requirements after caesarean section. COMT Val158Met did not alter acute analgesia or PPAC at 3 months
  • The extent of perineal trauma increases acute pain but has not been shown to increase PPAC

Q1.5Optimising analgesia after caesarean section, and does it prevent persistent pain?

  • Neuraxial anaesthesia with intrathecal morphine (50 to 100 micrograms, or epidural morphine 2 to 4 mg), plus a lipophilic opioid such as fentanyl. There is a ceiling effect, with more adverse effects at higher doses
  • Regular (scheduled) paracetamol and NSAID. They are synergistic, and scheduled is better than as-needed
  • Reserve opioids for breakthrough pain (oral first, IV only for severe pain) and consider breastfeeding and drug transfer into breast milk
  • If intrathecal morphine is not used: wound infiltration, or TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks
  • Adjuncts have not been shown to reduce PPAC:
    • Gabapentin: inconsistent acute benefit, no reduction in PPAC
    • Ketamine: reduces morphine use but not PPAC (S-ketamine may reduce early hyperalgesia)
    • Intrathecal clonidine: reduces incisional hyperalgesia at 48 hours, no change in PPAC at 6 months

Does it prevent persistent pain?

There is no proven risk-stratification model or pre-emptive treatment that reduces PPAC. Good analgesia still matters for recovery, bonding and postnatal mood.

Q1.6Burning pain and numbness at the Pfannenstiel scar 6 months after caesarean section

  • Assess for neuropathic pain: history, DN4, sensory mapping, Tinel's sign (medial to the ASIS). Screen for postnatal depression
  • Exclude other causes: incisional hernia, scar endometriosis (cyclical pain, palpable nodule), adhesions. Ultrasound the scar if needed
  • Topical: lidocaine 5% plaster
  • Anti-neuropathic agents
  • Ultrasound-guided ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric or TAP block with local anaesthetic, with or without steroid (diagnostic and therapeutic). Then pulsed radiofrequency or cryoneurolysis. Surgical neurectomy or scar revision for refractory entrapment
  • Psychology and physiotherapy support. Reassure her that persistent pain becomes less frequent with time, set functional goals, and liaise with the obstetric team and GP

SCQ 2Basic pharmacology of local anaesthetics

Q2.1Structure and function of the voltage-gated sodium channel

  • A large pore-forming alpha subunit with one or two beta subunits
  • Three states: resting (closed, about −70 mV), open on depolarisation (S4 moves outwards, sodium enters; threshold about −55 mV, peak about +40 mV), then inactivated. It returns to resting only after repolarisation
  • Subtypes: nine (Nav1.1 to Nav1.9). Nav1.7 and Nav1.8 in sensory neurones, Nav1.5 in the heart. All are blocked by local anaesthetics

Q2.2How local anaesthetics block nerve conduction

  • The unionised (lipophilic) form crosses the membrane and re-equilibrates inside the cell according to pKa and intracellular pH. The ionised form binds inside the channel
  • The binding site is reached mainly when the channel is open, so block increases with firing frequency (use-dependent block). Bound drug stabilises the inactivated state
  • Concentration-dependent effects: lower action potential peak, higher threshold, slower conduction, longer refractory period, and finally loss of conduction
  • They also act on potassium and calcium channels and G-protein-coupled receptors
  • Differential block: A-gamma and A-delta fibres are most susceptible and C fibres relatively resistant. Clinically, sympathetic block is greatest, then temperature and pinprick, then proprioception, then motor. Touch may persist with an adequate block

Q2.3Structure, and esters versus amides

  • Aromatic ring (lipophilic), intermediate link (ester or amide), tertiary amine (hydrophilic). They are amphipathic weak bases supplied as water-soluble hydrochloride salts. Longer carbon chains or a piperidine ring increase lipid solubility, potency and duration

  • Chirality: levobupivacaine and ropivacaine are the S-enantiomers, with less affinity for cardiac sodium and potassium channels, so they are less cardiotoxic. Ropivacaine (propyl group) is less lipid soluble and gives a more sensory-selective block

Q2.4Properties determining onset, potency and duration

  • pKa determines onset. Local anaesthetics are weak bases, so the closer the pKa is to tissue pH, the more unionised drug crosses the membrane. Lidocaine (pKa 7.8) is faster than bupivacaine (8.1). Henderson–Hasselbalch for a base: pKa − pH = log ([ionised] / [unionised])
  • Lipid solubility (partition coefficient) determines potency and volume of distribution. Small molecular weight speeds diffusion
  • Protein binding determines duration. Binding is mainly to alpha-1 acid glycoprotein. Hypoxia, hypercarbia and acidosis reduce binding and raise toxicity risk. Infants under 6 months have lower binding. Vasoactivity also matters (adrenaline prolongs the block)
  • Infected or inflamed tissue is acidic, which increases ionisation, slows onset and reduces effect. Bicarbonate is sometimes added to speed epidural onset

Q2.5Systemic absorption and maximum doses

  • Absorption depends on site, rate of injection, dose and vasoactivity. Peak plasma levels after a single dose, highest first: intrapleural, intercostal, caudal, lumbar epidural, brachial plexus, sciatic and femoral, subcutaneous

Q2.6Toxicity and its management

  • Systemic toxicity (LAST): perioral numbness, tinnitus, agitation, seizures, then arrhythmias, myocardial depression and cardiac arrest
  • Why bupivacaine is most cardiotoxic: it binds cardiac sodium channels with high affinity and dissociates slowly ("fast in, slow out"), accumulating in diastole and causing re-entry arrhythmias
  • Management (AAGBI): stop injecting, call for help, airway and 100% oxygen, benzodiazepine for seizures, prolonged CPR if needed
  • Intralipid 20%: 1.5 ml/kg bolus, then 15 ml/kg/h. Up to two repeat boluses and double the infusion rate if no improvement. Maximum 12 ml/kg
  • Methaemoglobinaemia: prilocaine (metabolite o-toluidine), also benzocaine and lidocaine. Risk rises above the maximum dose. Treat with methylene blue
  • Local toxicity: dose- and time-dependent neurotoxicity (rare), and chondrotoxicity with intra-articular use (ropivacaine less than bupivacaine)

Q2.7Local anaesthetics in chronic pain, and newer agents

  • IV lidocaine reduces spontaneous pain, allodynia and hyperalgesia in neuropathic pain. The effect is not explained by sodium channel block alone (anti-inflammatory and central actions)
  • Topical: lidocaine 5% plaster. EMLA is a eutectic mixture of 2.5% lidocaine and 2.5% prilocaine, with a lower melting point that allows a higher concentration on the skin. Also diagnostic and therapeutic nerve blocks
  • Name at least one novel agent:
    • Liposomal bupivacaine: Cochrane found low-quality evidence and no clear benefit
    • Neosaxitoxin: site-1 sodium channel blocker acting at the outer pore. Little cardiotoxicity because cardiac Nav1.5 is relatively resistant; prolongs block when combined with bupivacaine
    • QX-314: quaternary lidocaine that enters through TRPV1 channels, giving a sensory-selective block
    • Polymer depots such as HTX-011 (bupivacaine with meloxicam)

SCQ 3Advances in spinal cord stimulation

Q3.1How SCS relieves pain

  • Originally based on gate control theory: stimulation of large-diameter A-beta fibres in the dorsal columns
  • Antidromic signals activate GABAergic interneurones in the dorsal horn. GABA acts at pre- and postsynaptic GABA-A and GABA-B receptors to inhibit nociceptive transmission
  • Orthodromic signals reach brainstem nuclei, thalamus, primary somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate cortex and insula, recruiting descending serotonergic and noradrenergic inhibition of dorsal horn neurones
  • Newer paraesthesia-free waveforms probably work differently (dorsal horn interneurones, glial modulation)

Q3.3Closed-loop SCS and the evidence

  • Open-loop systems give a fixed output, so movement of the cord with posture, breathing or activity causes over- or under-stimulation
  • Closed-loop systems measure the evoked compound action potential (ECAP), the dorsal column response to each pulse, and adjust the output pulse by pulse to keep it at a target level, compensating for cord movement
  • EVOKE (double-blind RCT, closed-loop versus open-loop): higher responder rates, less disability, better quality of life and greater opioid reduction at 24 months. At 36 months, 77.6% of closed-loop participants had at least 50% pain reduction versus 49.3% on open-loop. EVOKE 36-month results
  • Avalon (open-label): 89.5% had at least 50% pain reduction at 2 years

Q3.4Dorsal root ganglion (DRG) stimulation, and when to use it

  • Leads are placed through the foramen onto the DRG, which contains the cell bodies of primary afferents. It modulates ectopic firing and restores the filtering function of the T-junction
  • Indication: focal, dermatomal neuropathic pain, such as lower-limb CRPS, groin pain, foot or knee pain. Paraesthesia is stable with posture and there are fewer off-target effects
  • ACCURATE (CRPS and causalgia of the lower limbs, DRG versus conventional SCS): treatment success 81.2% versus 55.7% at 3 months, and 74.2% versus 53.0% at 12 months, with better quality of life and less postural variation. ACCURATE, Pain 2017
  • Complications and difficulty: technically more demanding. Lead migration, CSF leak and infection. Previous spinal surgery can distort the anatomy

Q3.5Summarising the overall evidence

  • NICE TA159: SCS recommended for chronic neuropathic pain (at least 50 mm on a 0 to 100 mm VAS) for at least 6 months despite appropriate conventional medical management, after multidisciplinary team assessment and a successful trial. Not recommended for ischaemic pain except in research. NICE TA159
  • Outcomes depend on selection, smoking, psychological distress and follow-up

Q3.6Practical device advances

  • Rechargeable versus primary-cell IPG. Rechargeable lasts longer (median about 7 years), is smaller and cheaper overall. Primary-cell (median about 4 years) suits patients with cognitive or physical impairment, limited prognosis or poor follow-up access
  • MRI conditionality can be lost over time. In impedance-dependent systems, lead impedance rose beyond safe limits in 18.5% by about 2 years and 43% by 5 years. Check impedance before every scan, and choose impedance-independent systems for patients likely to need MRI
  • Other advances: wireless systems without an IPG (external transmitter), remote monitoring and programming, and machine learning models that predicted responders better than clinical judgement (about 73% versus 40%)

Q3.7Complications and patient selection

  • Hardware: lead migration, lead fracture, IPG pocket pain, battery failure, loss of efficacy (a common reason for explant)
  • Biological: infection (implant site under 1% in RELIEF), wound breakdown, haematoma, dural puncture and headache. Rarely epidural haematoma or abscess and neurological injury
  • Selection: neuropathic pain with an appropriate anatomical distribution, failure of conventional management, multidisciplinary assessment including psychological assessment (untreated depression, unrealistic expectations, substance misuse), stopping smoking, a trial of stimulation, and clear goals focused on function
